Brief

Two hours, 68 questions, and a section you cannot go back to

CAT is not a knowledge test. Everyone at the 99th percentile can solve every question given unlimited time. It is a selection problem under a hard clock: pick the right 50 of 68, in three 40-minute windows that lock behind you. That is market making with 22 instruments and no ability to re-quote.

68 Q24 VARC · 22 DILR · 22 QA
204max marks · +3 / −1 · TITA no penalty
40 minper section, sequential, no return
~99.2realistic target on 35 hours

The asymmetry that drives every decision on this page. 32 of 66 in DILR is roughly the 99th percentile — eleven correct, zero wrong. VARC needs about 49 of 72 for the same percentile. Most of the country cannot do DILR or QA at all; almost everyone can read. So your marginal hour is worth far more in VARC up to the sectional floor, and worth nothing above it.

Your edge

QA and DILR are close to free

  • QA is class 8–10 arithmetic, algebra and mensuration. No calculus, no proofs. Your only real risk is over-solving.
  • DILR is constraint propagation with a story on top. You do this for a living.
  • Arithmetic alone is ~45% of QA and the highest marks-per-minute content in the paper.
Your hole

VARC is where quant people die

  • Four dense passages on aesthetics, historiography, sociology. Not your reading.
  • The "correct" answer often feels arguable, so you burn time re-reading instead of committing.
  • IIM A/B/C impose sectional cutoffs near the 80–85th percentile. A 99.8 overall with a 75 in VARC gets rejected by all three.

Sixteen questions untouched and you land at 99.2. The VARC line is mediocre — 12 of 17 — and it still works, because DILR and QA carry the paper. Internalise that number. The people who try to attempt all 68 score 70.

The honest bit. IIM composite scores weight academic and gender diversity heavily. As a general-category male engineer, the practical bar for an A/B/C call is typically 99.5+, sometimes higher, even with strong academics and work experience. This page is optimised for a cleanly played 99+, not for calls. FMS, MDI, SPJIMR, the IIT DoMS programmes and the newer IIMs sit meaningfully lower.

Section 01

The 35 hours

Ten weeks from 20 September. Roughly 55% of the budget goes to mocks and mock analysis rather than to learning content — deliberate, because you have no knowledge gap. You have an exam-craft gap, and exam-craft is only built under the clock.

4hours

Phase 0 — Calibrate Week 1 · 20–27 Sep

  • Take CAT 2024 Slot 2 cold, full timing, 8:30 AM, no preparation. Three 40-minute alarms, stop dead on each.
  • Analyse for 90 minutes. Tag every miss: knowledge / slow / careless / selection / blind.
  • That diagnostic reallocates everything below. Default split is VARC 5h, DILR 4h, QA 2h.
  • VARC under 80th percentile → go VARC 7h, DILR 3h, QA 1h. This is the likely case and the emergency one.
11hours

Phase 1 — Build Weeks 2–4 · 28 Sep – 18 Oct

  • VARC, 5h. 15–18 real passages, 8 minutes each, hard stop, all four questions attempted. Then name the trap on every miss.
  • DILR, 4h. 16–20 sets, 12 minutes each, hard stop. On every failure ask one question only: what was the entry point I missed?
  • QA, 2h. One arithmetic speed session, one full sectional sim. The sim's purpose is practising abandonment, not scoring.
  • Two sessions a week, 1.5–2h each. Never 20-minute sessions — the section is 40 minutes and you need that endurance.
14hours

Phase 2 — Mock cycle Weeks 5–8 · 19 Oct – 15 Nov

  • One full mock per week, same time every week, Saturday or Sunday 8:30 AM. 2h mock + 1h15 analysis.
  • Buy a series (IMS / TIME / CL, ~₹2.5k). The percentile against a real cohort of 20,000 is the whole point; your raw score alone means nothing.
  • Week 6 focus: selection discipline. Week 7: deliberately do only 3 VARC passages plus all 8 VA and see if accuracy beats the rush. Week 8: full dress rehearsal.
  • The 20-minute selection audit inside each analysis is the highest-value block in the plan. Questions you skipped but could have solved in 60 seconds are usually the single biggest leak.
6hours

Phase 3 — Peak and taper Weeks 9–10 · 16–29 Nov

  • Sat 21 Nov: final mock at your actual allotted slot time, plus analysis. Then no more mocks.
  • One hour re-solving every question missed across all five mocks. The same three or four failure modes will repeat. Those are your real opponents.
  • No new material after 22 Nov. Logistics, 20-minute warm-ups every other day, re-read the playbook on Friday 27th.
  • Sat 28 Nov: nothing. Sleep. CAT is a sustained-attention test and sleep debt costs more than any two hours of revision gains.

If you end up with more than 35 hours — your stated weekly rate suggests nearer 50 — add in this order, ranked by marginal percentile per hour: two more mocks (+6h), twelve more RC passages (+4h), DILR set-selection drills (+3h), QA sectional sims (+2h). Do not spend them on advanced maths, vocabulary lists, grammar, or "completing the syllabus." CAT has no syllabus worth completing.

Section 02

Exam-day playbook

Read this three times: now, after Mock 2, and on Friday 27 November. −1 is the spread. Ninety seconds is the capital charge. The capital charge dominates by an order of magnitude.

The three things that lose you the exam.

1. Sunk-cost paralysis. Eight minutes into a DILR set that won't crack, you continue because you've already spent eight minutes. This is the most common way a strong candidate posts a bad DILR score.

2. Skipping questions you could have done. Every mock analysis will surface 3–5 questions you never opened that you'd have solved in 60 seconds. That's 9–15 marks, a full percentile band, left on the table because you never scanned.

3. Tilt after VARC. VARC comes first and it will feel bad. You will walk into DILR thinking the paper is gone. It isn't, and you have zero information — nobody knows their score mid-exam and everything is normalised within your slot.

VARC · 40 min · 24 Q

Target: 16–18 attempts, 11–13 correct, ~86th

  1. 0–2 min: read the first two lines of all four passages, rank them. Do the tractable one first.
  2. 2–26 min: three passages, 8 minutes each — 4 reading, 4 on questions.
  3. 26–36 min: all 8 VA questions. Para-jumble and odd-one-out are TITA, so never leave them blank.
  4. 36–40 min: skim passage 4 for the one or two direct questions, or finish VA properly instead.

Never leave a passage you've read without attempting all four questions. The read is the sunk cost; the questions are cheap once you've read. Three passages done well beats four rushed: 12/12 is 36 marks, 11/16 is 26.

DILR · 40 min · 22 Q · 4 sets

Target: 3 sets solved, 13–15 correct, ~99th

  1. 0–5 min: read all four sets, solve nothing. Mark each GO / MAYBE / NO. This is the best five minutes in the exam and almost everyone skips it.
  2. The test for GO is not "can I solve it" but can I see the first inference in 60 seconds.
  3. 5–17, 17–29, 29–40: three GO sets, twelve minutes each.

The 12-minute abandon rule. Write your start time for each set on the rough sheet so you can't lie to yourself. This will feel wrong in the moment and it is the most valuable line on this page.

Depth beats breadth here — the opposite of QA. Once a set cracks, the rest of its questions are nearly free.

QA · 40 min · 22 Q

Target: 17–19 attempts, 15–17 correct, ~99.5th

  1. Pass 1, 0–12 min: all 22 in order, solving only what you can see the path to within 10 seconds. Nets 8–10. Mark the rest M or X.
  2. Pass 2, 12–30 min: the M questions at 90–120 seconds each. Nets 5–7 more.
  3. Pass 3, 30–40 min: guess every remaining TITA (free), guess MCQs where two options are eliminated, re-check the two you felt rushed on.

Three-minute hard cap on any question. None. Not even the elegant one. A four-minute question you get right cost you two you'd also have gotten right: net −3.

Plug the options. Approximate aggressively. You are not graded on method.

If it goes wrong

Recovery lines

  • VARC felt like a disaster. It felt that way for everyone in your slot. Percentiles are normalised within slot. You have no information. Play DILR and QA exactly as planned.
  • No DILR set looks doable at minute 5. Take the two least-bad and commit. Two sets clean — 11 correct, 33 marks — is around the 99th percentile anyway.
  • Minute 30 in QA with 8 attempts. Stop solving. Sweep for arithmetic and TITA and harvest volume. Eight more at 70% beats three more at 100%.
  • Machine problem. Hand up immediately, don't troubleshoot. Lost time is compensated only if it's logged.
Section 03

VARC

One RC passage at real CAT density plus four verbal-ability items. Eight minutes for the passage, twelve for the rest. The autopsy matters more than the score: on every miss, name which clause supports the right answer and what made the wrong one attractive.

The five traps

CAT wrong options fail in stereotyped ways

TrapWhat it looks like
Too strongUses always, must, proves, impossible, abandon where the passage said tends to, suggests.
Half-rightFirst clause textually supported, second clause invented. You read the first and stopped.
Out of scopeTrue, sensible, and not in the passage.
ReversalStates the view the author is arguing against as if it were the author's.
Detail as main ideaAccurately describes paragraph 3, offered as the answer to "the primary purpose of the passage."

Over 15 passages, one or two of these will account for most of your misses. Naming it is most of the fix.

RC

Passage — on translation and untranslatability

8 min

Every act of translation begins with a loss that cannot be itemised. The translator does not merely substitute one word for another; she reconstructs, in a second language, an object whose shape was determined by the first. What the original achieved effortlessly — a pun that doubles as an argument, a cadence that enacts the hesitation it describes — must now be achieved by other means or abandoned. The question that has vexed theorists of translation for two centuries is whether this loss is incidental, a friction to be minimised by better technique, or constitutive, a feature of the enterprise itself.

The instrumentalist answer, dominant for most of the modern period, treats a text as a container for meaning. Meaning is the cargo; language is the vessel. On this view, translation is a transfer operation, and a good translation is one that delivers the cargo intact, with the vessel's peculiarities discarded as packaging. The appeal of this model is obvious: it makes translation assessable. One can ask whether the information arrived, and the answer is in principle a matter of fact.

Against this stands a tradition, running from Schleiermacher through Benjamin to Berman, that denies the separability of cargo and vessel. If the meaning of a text is not prior to its language but produced by it, then there is nothing to extract. What the translator confronts is not a message in a foreign wrapper but a form of life — a set of assumptions about what may be said, what must be implied, and what will be understood without saying. On this account, a translation that reads fluently in the target language has almost certainly falsified its source, because fluency is precisely the sensation of encountering one's own assumptions. The translator's obligation is therefore not to domesticate but to disturb: to make the target language strain visibly under the weight of what it is being asked to carry.

The difficulty with this second view is that it proves too much. If untranslatability is constitutive, translation should be impossible, and yet it manifestly is not. Texts do cross; arguments do survive the passage; readers of a translated philosopher can and do disagree productively with her. Any theory that cannot account for this ordinary success is describing something other than translation.

A more defensible position abandons the question of fidelity altogether. Translation is not a transfer but a performance — a reading made public and made permanent. The translator, like a musician working from a score, produces something that did not exist before and that could have been produced otherwise. We do not ask whether a performance of a sonata is faithful; we ask whether it is intelligent, whether it hears things in the score that we had not heard. The relevant failure is not infidelity but dullness. The relevant virtue is not accuracy but consequence: the capacity of the new text to be argued with, borrowed from, and translated again.

This reframing dissolves nothing. It relocates the argument from metaphysics to criticism, where it probably always belonged. What it costs is the comfort of a standard external to judgement. What it buys is a description of the practice that translators themselves would recognise.

V1

The primary purpose of the passage is to:

core
(a) demonstrate that translation between languages is ultimately impossible
(b) argue that the debate over fidelity should be replaced by a different standard of evaluation
(c) defend the instrumentalist model against its critics
(d) trace the historical development of theories of translation from Schleiermacher onward
Answer

(b). The passage moves through instrumentalism, its opponents, an objection to those opponents, and lands on "a more defensible position abandons the question of fidelity altogether," replacing it with a performance standard. That arc is the purpose.

(a) is the view the author explicitly rejects — "translation should be impossible, and yet it manifestly is not." (c) is a reversal trap: instrumentalism is described sympathetically for one paragraph, then left behind; describing is not defending. (d) is detail-as-main-idea — the names appear but no history is traced, they're compressed into one clause as a label.

V2

According to the passage, a fluent translation is suspect because:

(a) translators who aim at fluency tend to abridge difficult passages
(b) the target language is rarely capable of reproducing the original's effects
(c) the reader's sense of ease signals that the source's foreign assumptions have been replaced by familiar ones
(d) fluency indicates the translator prioritised style over meaning
Answer

(c). Directly supported: "fluency is precisely the sensation of encountering one's own assumptions."

(b) and (d) are out of scope — plausible claims about translation the passage never makes. (a) invents a mechanism nowhere present.

V3

The author says the Schleiermacher–Benjamin–Berman view "proves too much" in order to:

(a) suggest the instrumentalist model is correct after all
(b) show that the view's own logic would rule out an outcome that plainly occurs
(c) argue that Benjamin misunderstood Schleiermacher's position
(d) establish that untranslatability applies only to poetry, not argumentative prose
Answer

(b). "Proves too much" is a term of art: the argument's own logic entails a conclusion we know to be false. The author spells it out — if untranslatability were constitutive, translation would be impossible, but "texts do cross."

(a) is the trap for readers who assume rejecting one view means endorsing its opposite. The author rejects both and proposes a third. (c) and (d) are fabrication.

V4

Which of the following, if true, would most weaken the author's analogy between translation and musical performance?

core
(a) Some musicians perform from memory rather than from a written score.
(b) Audiences routinely disagree about which performance of a sonata is best.
(c) Readers of translations, unlike listeners to sonatas, typically have no access to the original and so cannot recognise an intelligent reading as such.
(d) Many working translators describe themselves as craftsmen rather than artists.
Answer

(c). The analogy rests on judging a performance by whether "it hears things in the score that we had not heard." That requires the audience to have the score. If translation readers don't, the criterion is unusable and the analogy collapses at exactly its load-bearing point.

(b) doesn't weaken anything — the author welcomes disagreement, having just said the cost of this position is "the comfort of a standard external to judgement." (a) and (d) are irrelevant detail.

This is the highest-value question type in CAT VARC and it is pure logic, not reading. To weaken an analogy you attack the property the analogy depends on, not its surface. Find the load-bearing claim before you look at the options.

V5

Para-summary — discounting

Economists have long assumed that people discount future rewards at a constant rate, so that a delay of one year is worth the same whether it begins today or a decade hence. Experimental evidence contradicts this: subjects who prefer ₹100 today to ₹110 tomorrow will nonetheless prefer ₹110 in 31 days to ₹100 in 30 days. The same one-day delay is judged differently depending on when it occurs. This pattern is not a random error; it is systematic and reproducible, and it implies that a person's ranking of options can reverse simply with the passage of time, without any new information arriving.

(a) Experiments show people are irrational about money, so models built on rationality should be abandoned.
(b) People value immediate rewards more than delayed ones, which explains why saving is difficult.
(c) Evidence that an identical delay is weighted differently at different points in time contradicts constant discounting and implies preferences can reverse without new information.
(d) Economists should conduct more experiments before assuming how people value future rewards.
Answer

(c). Carries all three moves: the evidence, the target (constant discounting), and the implication (reversal without new information).

(a) is too strong — "abandoned" appears nowhere; one assumption is attacked, not the enterprise. (b) is half-right: true of the setup, but it misses the actual finding, which is inconsistency across time, not mere impatience. (d) adds a recommendation the paragraph never makes.

Rule: summary options that tell someone what to do are almost always wrong unless the paragraph itself prescribes.

V6

Odd one out — maps

tita

Three of these form a coherent set. Which one does not belong?

  1. The map's earliest function was not to help travellers find their way but to assert that a territory existed and belonged to someone.
  2. Medieval European mappae mundi place Jerusalem at the centre, a decision about significance rather than geography.
  3. Colonial surveyors drew boundaries across regions they had never walked, and those lines later hardened into the borders of states.
  4. Modern satellite imaging has reduced the cost of producing an accurate map by several orders of magnitude.
Answer

4. Sentences 1–3 share a theme: maps as instruments of claim and authority rather than navigation. Sentence 4 is about cost and technology — a different register entirely.

The odd one out is rarely off-topic. It is usually on-topic in a different register: specific vs general, mechanism vs consequence, descriptive vs evaluative, historical vs contemporary. Look for the shift in what kind of claim is being made, not in subject matter.

V7

Para-jumble — the beauty contest

tita
  • A. The result is a market in which the price of a share reflects not what investors believe about a company, but what they believe other investors believe.
  • B. Keynes compared the stock market to a newspaper beauty contest in which readers win not by picking the prettiest face, but by picking the face most other readers will pick.
  • C. Each participant is therefore reasoning about the reasoning of others, and knows that the others are doing the same.
  • D. Under such conditions, information about the company itself can become almost irrelevant to short-horizon price movements.
Answer

BCAD. B opens — it introduces the analogy with no back-reference, so nothing can precede it. C follows with "therefore," drawing out the mechanism. A begins "The result is," which needs C's mechanism to be a result of. D begins "Under such conditions," extending the state A describes.

The reliable anchors are the sentence with no back-reference (it opens) and the explicit connectives: therefore, the result is, under such conditions, however, by contrast. Find the connective chain and the order falls out without your needing to understand the content. And since this is TITA with no negative marking, type any order rather than leave it blank.

V8

Para-summary — the jury

The standard defence of the jury is that it introduces the judgement of ordinary people into a system otherwise run by specialists. But this defence concedes the critics' premise: that jurors are amateurs whose value lies in their amateurism. A stronger case notes that jurors are not asked to interpret law, only to determine what happened, and that determining what happened is not a specialist skill. On matters of credibility, inference and ordinary human motive, twelve people deliberating have no systematic disadvantage against one trained lawyer, and a structural advantage in the diversity of experience they bring.

(a) Juries should be retained because ordinary citizens deserve a role in the legal system.
(b) The usual defence of juries concedes too much; the better argument is that fact-finding is not a specialist skill and deliberating groups are well suited to it.
(c) Jurors are better than judges at determining facts because they bring diverse life experiences.
(d) Critics of the jury system misunderstand the distinction between interpreting law and determining facts.
Answer

(b). The paragraph rejects the standard defence as conceding too much, then offers a better one. (b) is the only option carrying both moves.

(a) is the standard defence the paragraph argues against — reversal. (c) is too strong: the passage claims "no systematic disadvantage," not superiority. (d) is half-right — the law/fact distinction is real and present, but it's a premise in the argument, not the argument.

Where to get unlimited more, free. Past CAT papers from 2019–2025 are released officially at iimcat.ac.in and mirrored with solutions on Cracku, 2IIM and iQuanta. That's roughly 27 slots — about 1,800 questions at exactly the right difficulty, written by the people who write your exam. Pull three passages per session.

Background reading, free, doesn't count against the 35 hours: Aeon, Psyche, 3 Quarks Daily, The Browser, London Review of Books, The Caravan. CAT passages are drawn from exactly this register. Read the hard ones — reading Quanta because it's comfortable trains nothing.

Section 04

DILR

Three sets, 17 questions, 36 minutes. Spend the first four minutes reading all three and ranking them GO / MAYBE / NO — write the ranking down before you solve anything. Then twelve minutes per set, hard stop.

The taxonomy

Name the set type in ten seconds

Arrangement grid (people × attributes × positions) · tournament or round-robin with partial results · scheduling with precedence constraints · voting and elimination rounds with redistribution · network or flow (routes, capacities, costs) · partially filled table with totals plus a logical rider · Venn and set-overlap with min–max questions · games with optimal play.

Every set has exactly one entry point — the constraint that collapses the possibility space first. Finding it in 90 seconds is the whole skill. Everything after it is bookkeeping you can already do.

D1

Set 1 — The trading floor

6 Qgrid

Five traders — Arjun, Bela, Chirag, Divya, Esha — sit at desks numbered 1 to 5 left to right. Each covers exactly one of NIFTY, BANKNIFTY, RELIANCE, HDFC, INFY. Each has a distinct P&L from {−2, +1, +3, +5, +8} in ₹ lakh.

  1. Chirag sits at desk 3 and his P&L was +3.
  2. The INFY trader sits at desk 1.
  3. The trader at desk 5 had a P&L of +8.
  4. Divya was the only trader to lose money.
  5. Bela's P&L was higher than Arjun's, which was higher than Esha's.
  6. Divya sits adjacent to Chirag.
  7. The NIFTY trader sits immediately left of the BANKNIFTY trader.
  8. The RELIANCE trader had a P&L of +1.
  9. Bela does not cover HDFC.

1.1 Who covers HDFC?  ·  1.2 P&L of the trader at desk 2?  ·  1.3 Divya's desk?  ·  1.4 What does the trader immediately right of the RELIANCE trader cover?  ·  1.5 Combined P&L of desks 1 and 4?  ·  1.6 If clue 9 were removed, how many complete arrangements would be possible?

Answer

Entry point: clues 4 and 5 together. Divya is the only loss, so Divya = −2. Chirag = +3. That leaves {+1, +5, +8} for the other three and clue 5 orders them completely: Bela +8, Arjun +5, Esha +1. All five P&Ls are fixed before you place anyone. That's the collapse; everything after is forced.

  • Desk 5 has +8 → Bela at desk 5. RELIANCE has +1 → Esha covers RELIANCE.
  • Desk 1 is INFY. Chirag is at 3, Bela at 5, Esha covers RELIANCE not INFY, and Divya must be at 2 or 4. So desk 1 is Arjun, on INFY.
  • Divya at 2, Esha at 4: the only adjacent NIFTY/BANKNIFTY pair left is (2,3), forcing HDFC onto desk 5 = Bela. Violates clue 9. Dead.
  • Divya at 4, Esha at 2: the pair sits at (3,4) or (4,5). (3,4) again forces HDFC onto Bela. So it's (4,5), HDFC at desk 3.

D2

Set 2 — Round robin

5 Qtournament

Four teams — P, Q, R, S — play a round robin, every pair once. Win 3, draw 1 each, loss 0. Final tally: P = 7, Q = 4, R = 4, S = 1. It is also known that R did not draw with S.

2.1 How many matches were drawn?  ·  2.2 How many did S lose?  ·  2.3 Which team definitely lost to R?  ·  2.4 Which is certainly true: (a) Q drew with R (b) P beat Q (c) S drew with P (d) R lost to Q?  ·  2.5 In how many distinct ways could the six results have occurred?

Answer

Entry point: the points total, not the individual teams. Total = 16 across 6 matches. A decisive match puts 3 into the pot, a draw puts 2. So 3d + 2(6 − d) = 16 → d = 4 decisive, 2 drawn.

Decompose: P = 3+3+1 (2W 1D). Q = 3+1+0. R = 3+1+0. S = 1+0+0. Every team drew exactly once, so the two draws pair up all four teams. Three pairings exist; "R did not draw with S" kills (P–Q, R–S). The other two both check out:

Scenario AScenario B
DrawsP–S, Q–RP–R, Q–S
Pbeat Q, beat Rbeat Q, beat S
Qbeat S, lost to Pbeat R, lost to P
Rbeat S, lost to Pbeat S, lost to Q

2.1 2  ·  2.2 2  ·  2.3 S (R beats S in both)  ·  2.4 (b) P beat Q — true in both; (a) and (c) hold only in A, (d) only in B  ·  2.5 2.

Lesson: an under-determined set is not a broken set. CAT builds these deliberately and then asks "must be true" and "cannot be determined" against them. Enumerate every valid scenario and keep them all live. Candidates who assume they've misread something burn five minutes re-reading and then abandon a set they had already solved.

Section 05

QA

Full sectional simulation: 22 questions, 40 minutes, three-pass method. Q14, Q20 and Q22 are deliberate time sinks. A good run leaves four or five questions untouched — the point of this set is practising abandonment, not scoring.

Q1

False weight plus markup and discount

A shopkeeper uses a 900 g weight while claiming to sell one kilogram. He also marks goods 25% above cost and then offers a 10% discount on the marked price. Overall profit percentage?

(a) 12.5%
(b) 15%
(c) 22.5%
(d) 25%
Answer

(d) 25%. Cost = ₹1/g. The customer pays for 1 kg: MP = 1250, SP = 0.9 × 1250 = 1125. Goods handed over = 900 g, costing 900. Profit = 225/900 = 25%.

The two cheats compound on different bases — the weight cheat on quantity, the markup and discount on price. Don't add 25% and 12.5%.

Q2

Two trains, meeting point

tita

Two trains leave A and B simultaneously towards each other and meet after 6 hours. The train from A then takes 4 more hours to reach B. How many more hours after the meeting does the train from B take to reach A?

Answer

9. Before meeting, the A-train covers 6u and has 6v left, taking 6v/u = 4, so v/u = 2/3. The B-train has 6u left, taking 6u/v = 9.

Memorise the shortcut: meet after t, then take a and b more hours → t = √(ab). Here 6 = √(4b) → b = 9. A 90-second question becomes a 10-second one.

Q3

A leaves, B finishes

A and B together finish a job in 12 days. They work together 5 days, A leaves, and B completes the rest in 21 days. How long would A alone take?

(a) 15
(b) 18
(c) 20
(d) 24
Answer

(b) 18. Combined rate 1/12. Five days leaves 7/12. B does 7/12 in 21 days → B = 1/36. A = 1/12 − 1/36 = 1/18.

Q4

Milk and water, draw and replace

tita

80 litres of milk–water in ratio 5:3. Some mixture is drawn out and replaced by pure water; the ratio becomes 1:1. How many litres were drawn out?

Answer

16. Milk starts at 50; volume stays 80, so a 1:1 final ratio means milk = 40. Drawing x litres removes 5x/8 of milk: 50 − 5x/8 = 40 → x = 16.

Q5

Maximise the second largest

tita

Five distinct positive integers average 20. The largest is 40. Maximum possible value of the second largest?

Answer

39. Sum = 100. Distinctness caps the second largest at 39; check feasibility — the other three must be distinct positive integers summing to 21, e.g. 1 + 2 + 18.

Classic maximise-one-by-minimising-the-rest, but you must verify feasibility. Blindly setting the bottom three to 1, 2, 3 gives 54, which violates "largest = 40."

Q6

Consecutive-integer roots

Both roots of x² − px + q = 0 are consecutive integers. Which must be true?

(a) p² − 4q = 1
(b) p² = 4q
(c) p² − 4q = 2
(d) p = q
Answer

(a). p² − 4q is the discriminant, which equals (difference of roots)² = 1² = 1. Two seconds if you see it; 40 if you expand (2n+1)² − 4n(n+1).

Q7

Three logs, one base

tita

log₂x + log₄x + log₁₆x = 7. Find x.

Answer

16. With t = log₂x: t(1 + ½ + ¼) = 7 → (7/4)t = 7 → t = 4.

Q8

Sum of two moduli

tita

How many integer values of x satisfy |x − 3| + |x + 2| ≤ 9?

Answer

10. The expression is the sum of distances from 3 and −2, minimum 5 on [−2, 3]. Right of 3: 2x − 1 ≤ 9 → x ≤ 5. Left of −2: 1 − 2x ≤ 9 → x ≥ −4. So x ∈ [−4, 5].

Q9

Last two digits of 7¹⁰³

tita

Remainder when 7¹⁰³ is divided by 100?

Answer

43. 7¹ = 7, 7² = 49, 7³ ≡ 43, 7⁴ ≡ 1 (mod 100). Cycle 4; 103 ≡ 3.

Q10

Perfect-square factors

tita

How many factors of 2⁵ × 3⁶ × 5² are perfect squares?

Answer

24. Even exponents only: {0,2,4} × {0,2,4,6} × {0,2} = 3 × 4 × 2.

Q11

Altitude in a 13-14-15 triangle

In triangle ABC, AB = 13, BC = 14, CA = 15. Length of the altitude from A to BC?

(a) 11
(b) 12
(c) 12.5
(d) 13
Answer

(b) 12. Heron: s = 21, area = √7056 = 84, so 84 = ½(14)h.

13-14-15 is a standard CAT triangle — two 3-4-5-family right triangles glued along an altitude of 12 (splitting the base into 5 and 9). Recognise it and skip Heron entirely.

Q12

Inradius of a right triangle

A circle is inscribed in a right triangle with legs 6 and 8. Radius?

(a) 1.5
(b) 2
(c) 2.4
(d) 3
Answer

(b) 2. For a right triangle, r = (a + b − c)/2 = (6 + 8 − 10)/2.

Q13

Cylinder into spheres

tita

A solid cylinder of radius 3 and height 8 is melted and recast into spheres of radius 1. How many complete spheres?

Answer

54. 72π ÷ (4π/3).

Q14

PERMUTE, no two vowels adjacent

time sinktita

In how many ways can the letters of PERMUTE be arranged so that no two vowels are adjacent?

Answer

720. Consonants P, R, M, T → 4! = 24. Five gaps; choose 3 and place E, E, U one per gap: C(5,3) × 3!/2! = 10 × 3 = 30. Total 24 × 30.

The repeated E is the trap — dividing by 2! at the wrong stage is the standard error. On a first pass this is a skip: slow and error-prone for the same 3 marks as Q1.

Q15

Conditional on an even sum

Two fair dice. Given the sum is even, probability both show the same number?

(a) 1/6
(b) 1/4
(c) 1/3
(d) 1/2
Answer

(c) 1/3. Even sum = both odd (9) or both even (9) = 18. Doubles = 6, all with even sums. 6/18.

Q16

AP from its sum

tita

The sum of the first n terms of an AP is 3n² + 5n. Its 10th term?

Answer

62. aₙ = Sₙ − Sₙ₋₁ = 6n + 2.

Q17

A function composed three times

f(x) = x/(x − 1) for x ≠ 1. Then f(f(f(x))) equals:

(a) x
(b) x/(x − 1)
(c) (x − 1)/x
(d) 1/(1 − x)
Answer

(b). f(f(x)) = [x/(x−1)] ÷ [1/(x−1)] = x, so f is its own inverse, f² is the identity, and f³ = f.

Or plug x = 3: f(3) = 1.5, f(1.5) = 3, f(3) = 1.5. Only (b) gives 1.5. Plugging beats algebra on most MCQ function questions.

Q18

Boat, stream, round trip

tita

A boat covers 30 km downstream and 30 km back in 8 hours. Stream speed 2 km/h. Speed of the boat in still water?

Answer

8 km/h. 30/(b+2) + 30/(b−2) = 8 → 2b² − 15b − 8 = 0 → b = 8.

Faster: try sensible integers. b = 8 gives 3 + 5 = 8. Fifteen seconds. Don't reach for the quadratic when the answer is a small integer.

Q19

Doubling to eightfold

A sum at compound interest doubles in 8 years. In how many years is it eight times?

(a) 16
(b) 20
(c) 24
(d) 32
Answer

(c) 24. 8 = 2³, so three doublings. Compound growth is multiplicative — do not scale linearly.

Q20

Half a rotated square

time sinktita

Area of the region satisfying both |x| + |y| ≤ 4 and x ≥ 0?

Answer

16. |x| + |y| ≤ 4 is a square rotated 45° with both diagonals 8, area = ½(8)(8) = 32. x ≥ 0 takes exactly half by symmetry.

Q21

Class average, split by ratio

Boys to girls is 3:2. Boys average 68, the class averages 72. Girls' average?

(a) 74
(b) 76
(c) 78
(d) 80
Answer

(c) 78. Alligation is fastest: boys are 4 below the mean and the ratio is 3:2, so girls are (3/2)(4) = 6 above. 72 + 6 = 78.

Q22

Reciprocals summing to 1/12

time sinktita

How many ordered pairs of positive integers (a, b) satisfy 1/a + 1/b = 1/12?

Answer

15. Rearranges to (a − 12)(b − 12) = 144. Both factors must be positive — if both were negative, a and b are under 12 and the largest product is 11 × 11 = 121 < 144. So the count is the number of divisors of 144 = 2⁴ × 3², i.e. 5 × 3 = 15.

The post-mortem that matters. If you took Q14, Q20 and Q22 on the first pass and ran out of time for Q1, Q3 and Q21 — which are 60-second arithmetic questions — that is a selection failure, not a maths failure, and it is the single most important thing this set can teach you. Also record your longest single question. If it's over three minutes, that's your finding regardless of score.

Tag every miss

Five tags, and what each one means

  • Knowledge — didn't know the concept. Should be near zero in QA. If not, name the topic and fix it directly.
  • Slow — knew it, ran out of time. Arithmetic drills; stop writing full working.
  • Careless — knew it, executed wrong. Each one is a 4-mark swing, +3 to −1. The most expensive category.
  • Selection — shouldn't have opened it. Re-read the playbook and run set-selection drills.
  • Blind — read it, no idea where to start. More set-type exposure, fast, abandoning early.
The analysis, 75 minutes

Same day, never skipped

  1. 15 min — re-tag every miss, log the counts.
  2. 20 min — the selection audit, ignoring right and wrong. Which questions did I open that I should have passed on? Which did I skip that I'd have solved in 60 seconds? Did I read all four DILR sets first? Did I abandon at 12 minutes? How long was my longest QA question?
  3. 25 min — re-solve everything missed, on paper. Not read. Solved.
  4. 15 min — write three concrete behavioural rules for next time. Not "be more careful."

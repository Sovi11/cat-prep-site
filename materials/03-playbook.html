Exam-Day Playbook

Read this three times before 29 November. Once now, once after Mock 2, once on Friday 27th.

The governing idea

You are not being tested on whether you can solve the questions. You can. So can everyone else in the 99th percentile. You are being tested on whether you can pick the right 50 out of 68 inside two hours.

Reframe each section as a 40-minute trading session:

  • A fixed inventory of 22–24 instruments, of wildly varying quality
  • You can only fill a fraction of them
  • −1 is the spread. Ninety seconds is the capital charge. The capital charge dominates by an order of magnitude.
  • The worst trade in CAT is a four-minute question you get right. It scored +3 and cost you two other questions you'd also have gotten right. Net −3.
  • There is no prize for inventory. Sixteen untouched questions is a winning paper.

The EV algebra on a 4-option MCQ: take it if 3p − (1−p) > 0, i.e. p > 0.25. So eliminating even one option makes a guess technically +EV. But EV is not your binding constraint and you should mostly ignore this. Time is the constraint. Guess when the clock is dying, and always on TITA.

The three things that lose you the exam

  1. Sunk-cost paralysis. Eight minutes into a DILR set that won't crack, you keep going because you've already invested eight minutes. This is the single most common way a strong candidate posts a bad DILR score.
  2. Skipping questions you could have done. In every mock analysis, you will find 3–5 questions you never opened that you'd have solved in 60 seconds. That is 9–15 marks — a full percentile band — sitting on the table because you never scanned.
  3. Tilt after VARC. VARC comes first and it will feel bad. You will walk into DILR thinking the paper is gone. It isn't, and you have no information — nobody knows their score mid-exam, and everything is normalised against your slot.

VARC — 40 minutes, 24 questions

Target: 16–18 attempts, 11–13 correct, ~30–36 raw, ~85–92 percentile. That clears every sectional cutoff. You are not trying to win this section; you are trying to not lose the exam in it.

The plan

Minutes 0–2: triage. Do not start reading passage 1. Read only the first two lines of each of the four passages and the topic of the 8 VA questions. Rank the passages 1–4 by how tractable they look. Then do them in that order.

You will get roughly this mix: one science/economics/technology passage (fast for you), one history/politics passage (medium), one philosophy/aesthetics/literary-theory passage (slow), one sociology/anthropology passage (medium). Do the fast one first — it banks marks and settles your nerves before the hard one.

Minutes 2–26: three passages, 8 minutes each. Per passage: ~4 minutes reading, ~4 minutes on the four questions.

Minutes 26–36: all 8 VA questions. These are worth doing: - Para-summary — MCQ, moderate. The answer is almost always the option that captures the paragraph's argument, not its topic. Eliminate anything that adds a recommendation the paragraph didn't make, or that overstates. - Odd-one-out — TITA, no negative marking. Find the theme in 3 of the 4–5 sentences; the odd one is usually in a different register (specific vs general, mechanism vs consequence, descriptive vs evaluative). - Para-jumble — TITA, no negative marking. Anchor on the opening sentence (introduces a term without a back-reference) and on connectives (therefore, the result is, under such conditions). If you can't crack it in 90 seconds, type any order and move on — free lottery ticket.

Minutes 36–40: passage 4. Skim it and take the one or two questions that are direct/factual. Or, if you're behind, use these four minutes to finish VA properly. Finishing VA beats a rushed fourth passage almost every time.

Non-negotiable rules

  • Never leave a passage having read it without attempting all four questions. The read is the sunk cost. The questions are cheap once you've read. Half-reading two passages is the worst possible allocation.
  • Three passages done well beats four passages rushed. 12/12 on three passages is 36 marks. 11/16 on four rushed passages is 26 marks. Test this explicitly in Mock 3.
  • Never re-read the whole passage to answer a question. Go to the paragraph the question points at.
  • On "primary purpose" and "main idea" questions, the answer must cover the whole passage. An option that accurately describes one paragraph is the standard trap.

DILR — 40 minutes, 22 questions, 4 sets

Target: 3 sets fully solved, 16–17 attempts, 13–15 correct, ~38–42 raw, ~99 percentile.

This is your section. It is also the section with the highest variance, and the variance is almost entirely down to selection.

The plan

Minutes 0–5: read all four sets. Solve nothing.

This is the single best five minutes in the entire exam and most candidates skip it. For each set, write one word on your rough sheet: GO / MAYBE / NO.

The test for GO: can I see the first inference? Not "can I solve it" — can you identify the constraint that collapses the possibility space, and see what it forces? If yes in 60 seconds, it's a GO. If you're still staring, it's a NO, today, for you. That's not a judgement on the set; it's a judgement on the match.

Also weigh: 6-question sets beat 5-question sets at equal difficulty. A set with a clean numeric table beats one with a wall of prose.

Minutes 5–17: GO set #1. Minutes 17–29: GO set #2. Minutes 29–40: GO set #3, or mop up single questions from the MAYBE sets.

Non-negotiable rules

  • The 12-minute abandon rule. If a set isn't cracked at 12:00, leave it. Write the time you started each set on your rough sheet so you can't lie to yourself. This rule will feel wrong in the moment and it is the most valuable line in this document.
  • Depth beats breadth in DILR — the exact opposite of QA. Three sets at 90% accuracy is ~99 percentile. Four sets half-attempted is ~85. Once a set is cracked, the remaining questions are nearly free, so the marginal value of finishing a cracked set is enormous.
  • Build the full solution before answering anything. The temptation is to answer question 1 as soon as you can. Resist it — fill the whole grid first, then answer all 5–6 in 90 seconds.
  • When a set is under-determined, don't panic — that's deliberate. Enumerate the 2–3 valid scenarios and answer "must be true" / "cannot be determined" against all of them. CAT loves this structure.
  • Rough sheet discipline. One clean grid per set, drawn once, filled in pen. Scrawling costs you more time than it saves.

QA — 40 minutes, 22 questions

Target: 17–19 attempts, 15–17 correct, ~44–48 raw, ~99.5+ percentile.

Your free section. The only way you lose marks here is by over-solving.

Three passes

Pass 1 — minutes 0–12: the sub-60-second questions. Go through all 22 in order. If you can see the path to the answer within ~10 seconds of reading, solve it now. If not, move on immediately. You should finish this pass having solved 8–10 questions. Mark the rest: M (medium, come back) or X (skip).

Pass 2 — minutes 12–30: the M questions. 90–120 seconds each. You should net 5–7 more.

Pass 3 — minutes 30–40: cleanup. - Any X question that's TITA: give it your best guess. Zero downside. - Any X MCQ where you can eliminate two options: guess. - Otherwise leave it blank and stop. - Spend the last two minutes re-checking the two questions where you felt rushed. Careless errors in QA are the most expensive thing in your paper, because they turn a +3 into a −1: a 4-mark swing per error.

Non-negotiable rules

  • The 3-minute hard cap. No question gets more than three minutes. None. Not even the elegant one.
  • Arithmetic first. If you're behind on time, scan for ratio / percentage / profit-loss / TSD / time-work / mixtures questions and take those. Highest marks per minute in the exam.
  • Plug the options. It's an MCQ. For quadratics, number-theory and many algebra questions, substituting the four options is faster than solving. You are not being graded on method.
  • Use approximation aggressively. If the options are 18%, 25%, 31%, 40%, you do not need three decimal places.
  • Geometry and modern maths (P&C, probability) are lower yield per minute. Take them in Pass 2, not Pass 1.

The two hours, logistically
The night before Sleep. Nothing else. No revision.
Morning Eat properly. Nothing heavy, nothing new. Normal caffeine dose — today is not the day to change it in either direction.
At the centre Arrive 90 minutes early. Biometrics and verification take real time and the queue is long. Carry the admit card printout and the exact original photo ID you registered with.
Minute 0 VARC starts. It will feel bad. That's the design.
Between sections Three slow breaths. Do not review. You cannot go back and the score is unknowable.
Minute 40 DILR. Five minutes of reading before you solve anything. This is the moment the whole exam turns on.
Minute 80 QA. Pass 1 is a sprint. Go.
After Don't discuss the paper. Don't check answer keys. Nothing you learn is actionable.

If something goes wrong

VARC felt like a disaster. Almost certainly it felt that way for everyone in your slot, and percentiles are normalised within slot. You have zero information. The correct response is to assume it was hard for everyone and play DILR and QA exactly as planned. Candidates who tilt after VARC lose 5–10 percentile points in the sections they were good at. This is by far the most common failure mode of strong candidates.

No DILR set looks doable after 5 minutes. Pick the two least-bad and commit. Even two sets solved cleanly (11 correct, ~33 marks) is around the 99th percentile in DILR. You are fine.

You're at minute 30 in QA with only 8 attempts. Stop trying to solve. Sweep for the remaining arithmetic and TITA questions and harvest volume. Eight more attempts at 70% accuracy beats three more at 100%.

Technical issue with the machine. Raise your hand immediately, don't try to fix it. Lost time is compensated at the centre, but only if it's logged.

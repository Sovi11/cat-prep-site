VARC — Practice Set #1

8 questions · 20 minutes One RC passage (4 questions, 8 minutes) + 4 Verbal Ability questions (12 minutes).

This is a deliberately hard passage — abstract, argumentative, humanities. Exactly the register you will lose marks on. Do it under the clock.

The protocol that matters is the autopsy, not the score. After you check your answers, for every miss write two sentences: 1. Which specific word or clause in the passage supports the right answer. 2. What made the wrong option attractive to you.

The second sentence is the whole exercise.

Reading Comprehension (8 minutes)

Every act of translation begins with a loss that cannot be itemised. The translator does not merely substitute one word for another; she reconstructs, in a second language, an object whose shape was determined by the first. What the original achieved effortlessly — a pun that doubles as an argument, a cadence that enacts the hesitation it describes — must now be achieved by other means or abandoned. The question that has vexed theorists of translation for two centuries is whether this loss is incidental, a friction to be minimised by better technique, or constitutive, a feature of the enterprise itself.

The instrumentalist answer, dominant for most of the modern period, treats a text as a container for meaning. Meaning is the cargo; language is the vessel. On this view, translation is a transfer operation, and a good translation is one that delivers the cargo intact, with the vessel's peculiarities discarded as packaging. The appeal of this model is obvious: it makes translation assessable. One can ask whether the information arrived, and the answer is in principle a matter of fact.

Against this stands a tradition, running from Schleiermacher through Benjamin to Berman, that denies the separability of cargo and vessel. If the meaning of a text is not prior to its language but produced by it, then there is nothing to extract. What the translator confronts is not a message in a foreign wrapper but a form of life — a set of assumptions about what may be said, what must be implied, and what will be understood without saying. On this account, a translation that reads fluently in the target language has almost certainly falsified its source, because fluency is precisely the sensation of encountering one's own assumptions. The translator's obligation is therefore not to domesticate but to disturb: to make the target language strain visibly under the weight of what it is being asked to carry.

The difficulty with this second view is that it proves too much. If untranslatability is constitutive, translation should be impossible, and yet it manifestly is not. Texts do cross; arguments do survive the passage; readers of a translated philosopher can and do disagree productively with her. Any theory that cannot account for this ordinary success is describing something other than translation.

A more defensible position abandons the question of fidelity altogether. Translation is not a transfer but a performance — a reading made public and made permanent. The translator, like a musician working from a score, produces something that did not exist before and that could have been produced otherwise. We do not ask whether a performance of a sonata is faithful; we ask whether it is intelligent, whether it hears things in the score that we had not heard. The relevant failure is not infidelity but dullness. The relevant virtue is not accuracy but consequence: the capacity of the new text to be argued with, borrowed from, and translated again.

This reframing dissolves nothing. It relocates the argument from metaphysics to criticism, where it probably always belonged. What it costs is the comfort of a standard external to judgement. What it buys is a description of the practice that translators themselves would recognise.

Economists have long assumed that people discount future rewards at a constant rate, so that a delay of one year is worth the same whether it begins today or a decade hence. Experimental evidence contradicts this: subjects who prefer ₹100 today to ₹110 tomorrow will nonetheless prefer ₹110 in 31 days to ₹100 in 30 days. The same one-day delay is judged differently depending on when it occurs. This pattern is not a random error; it is systematic and reproducible, and it implies that a person's ranking of options can reverse simply with the passage of time, without any new information arriving.

  (a) Experiments show that people are irrational about money, so economic models built on rationality should be abandoned.   (b) People value immediate rewards more highly than delayed ones, which explains why saving is difficult for most households.   (c) Evidence that an identical delay is weighted differently at different points in time contradicts constant discounting and implies that preferences can reverse without new information.   (d) Economists should conduct more experiments before making assumptions about how people value future rewards.

SOLUTIONS

Reading Comprehension

1. (b). The passage moves through instrumentalism, its opponents, an objection to those opponents, and lands on "a more defensible position abandons the question of fidelity altogether" — replacing it with a performance standard. That arc is the purpose.

  • (a) is the view the author explicitly rejects: "translation should be impossible, and yet it manifestly is not."
  • (c) is a reversal trap. Instrumentalism is described sympathetically for one paragraph, then left behind. Describing a view is not defending it.
  • (d) is a detail-as-main-idea trap. The names appear, but no history is traced — they're compressed into a single clause and used as a label for a position.

2. (c). Directly supported: "fluency is precisely the sensation of encountering one's own assumptions." The reader feels at ease because the foreign has been replaced by the familiar.

  • (b) and (d) are plausible-sounding claims about translation that the passage never makes. Classic out of scope: true-ish, sensible, not in the text.
  • (a) invents a mechanism (abridgement) nowhere present.

3. (b). "Proves too much" is a term of art: the argument's own logic entails a conclusion we know to be false. The author spells it out — if untranslatability were constitutive, translation would be impossible, but "texts do cross."

  • (a) is the trap for readers who think rejecting one view means endorsing its opposite. The author rejects both and proposes a third.
  • (c) and (d) are pure fabrication.

4. (c). The analogy rests on the claim that we judge a performance by whether "it hears things in the score that we had not heard." That judgement requires the audience to have access to the score. If translation readers don't have the original, the criterion is unusable and the analogy collapses at exactly its load-bearing point.

  • (b) doesn't weaken anything — the author welcomes disagreement, having just said the cost of this position is "the comfort of a standard external to judgement."
  • (a) and (d) are irrelevant details about musicians and translators that leave the argument untouched.

Note on Q4: this is the highest-value question type in CAT VARC and it is pure logic, not reading. To weaken an analogy you attack the property the analogy depends on, not the analogy's surface. Your background makes this question type free — but only if you slow down enough to identify what the argument actually rests on. Practise finding the load-bearing claim before you look at the options.

Verbal Ability

5. (c). Captures the evidence (identical delay weighted differently), the target (constant discounting), and the implication (preference reversal without new information) — all three moves the paragraph makes.

  • (a) too strong. "Abandoned" appears nowhere; the paragraph attacks one assumption, not the enterprise.
  • (b) is half-right: true of the setup, but it misses the actual finding, which is about inconsistency across time, not mere impatience.
  • (d) adds a recommendation the paragraph doesn't make. Summary options that tell someone what to do are almost always wrong unless the paragraph itself prescribes.

6. 4. Sentences 1, 2 and 3 share a theme: maps as instruments of claim and authority rather than navigation. Sentence 4 is about cost and technology — a different register entirely (economic/technical, not political).

The odd one out is rarely off-topic. It's usually on-topic in a different register: specific vs general, mechanism vs consequence, descriptive vs evaluative, historical vs contemporary. Look for the shift in what kind of claim is being made, not in subject matter.

7. BCAD. - B opens: it introduces the Keynes analogy with no back-reference, and nothing else can precede it. - C follows with "therefore," drawing out the mechanism implied by the beauty-contest setup. - A begins "The result is" — it needs the mechanism in C to be a result of something. - D begins "Under such conditions," extending the state of affairs A describes.

The reliable anchors in a para-jumble are (i) the sentence with no back-reference — it opens; and (ii) explicit connectives: therefore, the result is, under such conditions, however, by contrast. Find the chain of connectives and the order usually falls out without your having to understand the content.

8. (b). The paragraph does two things: rejects the standard defence as conceding too much, then offers a better one (fact-finding isn't specialist; groups are well suited). (b) is the only option carrying both moves.

  • (a) is the standard defence the paragraph is arguing against. Reversal trap.
  • (c) is too strong — the passage claims "no systematic disadvantage," not superiority.
  • (d) is half-right: the law/fact distinction is real and in the paragraph, but it's a premise in the argument, not the argument.

Post-mortem
